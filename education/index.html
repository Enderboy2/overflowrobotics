01 — The gap

Egypt educates millions. Industry still can't find job-ready talent.

Egypt runs the largest school system in the region and is investing heavily in skills-based reform. But hands-on engineering experience still reaches very few students — and usually too late.

The ratio

1 in 5,000

Roughly one Egyptian school student in five thousand sits in a dedicated STEM school. Obour STEM School alone takes about 150 of 30,000 applicants a year.

A

Degrees aren't the bottleneck. Experience is.

In Q3 2025, 43.4% of unemployed Egyptians held a university or postgraduate degree. Employers aren't short of graduates — they're short of graduates who have already worked on real problems.

B

Real work comes too late.

Most students meet a real engineering task for the first time in a final-year internship, if at all. By then their habits, interests and career choices are already set.

C

Edtech fills screens, not workbenches.

Most digital learning delivers content: videos, question banks, tablets. Very little of it puts real tools and real industry problems into a teenager's hands. That's the gap TIS is built to close.

02 — Start early

Why start before university?

The habits that make a good engineer — breaking a problem down, testing an idea, learning from failure — are easiest to build while students are still deciding who they want to become.

Better career choices

Students who have actually built and coded something choose their university path from experience, not guesswork.

Habits formed early

Planning, testing and documenting become second nature years before they're graded on them at university.

Resilience

When failure is treated as data, students stop fearing it. They learn to debug code, circuits and their own thinking.

Stronger school results

Physics, maths and programming make more sense once you've seen them move a robot.

A real portfolio

Projects, notebooks, certificates and competition entries that stand out on university and scholarship applications.

Teamwork & communication

Rotating team roles, explaining trade-offs and presenting results — the skills employers say graduates lack.

03 — How we teach

Project-based, every session.

No slide lectures. Every session is built around a challenge students solve with their own hands, coached by an engineer.

  1. 1Hands on the kit within 15 minutes
  2. 2Students hold the tools, not the instructor
  3. 3Failure is data
  4. 4Real engineering habits from day one
  5. 5Every session ends with something that works

How a session runs

  • 1Warm-upRecall question and prediction
  • 2Mini-lessonShort live demo of the concept needed
  • 3PlanSketch, pseudocode, roles and criteria
  • 4BuildStudents wire, code or assemble
  • 5Test & improveControlled test, one change, record
  • 6Show & tellDemo and explain one decision
  • 7ReflectNotebook entry and kit check

If a session runs behind, we cut decoration and extensions — never safety, planning or reflection.

The engineering notebook

Think like an engineer, on paper first.

The notebook is at the heart of TIS. For every project, students define the problem, explain their design choices, log the build, record test data — and reflect honestly on what failed and why. Instructors grade it as part of every project.

  1. 1

    Problem

    What problem are you solving? What does success look like, and how will you measure it?

  2. 2

    Research

    What did you look up or learn before building? Note your sources.

  3. 3

    Design & sketches

    What options did you consider? Which did you choose and why? Add sketches or photos.

  4. 4

    Build log

    What did you build, step by step? Include dates and photos of progress.

  5. 5

    Testing results

    How did you test it? Record what happened — numbers, tables, observations.

  6. 6

    What went wrong

    What failed or surprised you, and why do you think it happened?

  7. 7

    What I'd change

    If you built it again, what would you do differently?

The Minor Co-op Program

Real industry tasks for Egypt's next engineers — years before they reach university.

A Minor Co-op is a defined task a partner company gives to TIS student teams. It can be live, upcoming or already solved: the value is in the experience. Students finish it within an agreed time and present it to the company's engineers. It's unpaid, supervised by TIS instructors, and happens before students even reach university.

  1. 1

    You share a brief

    A real task: current, future or already completed.

  2. 2

    We map it

    TIS matches it to a module, track and student level.

  3. 3

    Teams build

    Over a few weeks or a full module.

  4. 4

    They document

    Build logs, test results and a final presentation.

  5. 5

    You review

    Your engineers judge the work and give feedback.

Questions

Frequently asked

Does my child need experience?

No. Module 1 starts from zero and adds extension challenges for students who move faster.

My school isn't a TIS partner. Can my child still join?

Yes. Enroll from home: order the Module 1 kit, join live online sessions with an Overflow instructor and use every feature of the TIS portal. You'll need a laptop or computer and a table to build on.

How does TIS work inside a school?

Overflow instructors run the sessions on site. Students work in small teams with one robot kit per team, and every student gets their own TIS portal account from us — no sign-up needed.

How are students assessed?

Each project is reviewed against a rubric — build quality, functionality, code, notebook and engineering reasoning — with written feedback. Parents see grades, attendance and feedback in the portal.

What if my child misses a session?

Every live session is recorded and posted on the course page.

Can our school partner with TIS?

Yes — we run school tours and in-school programmes. Email us to arrange a visit.
